## 1. Agent evaluation and benchmarks
Source: `course/site/notes/agent-evaluation.html` (mirror: `course/notes/notes_agent-evaluation.md`)

- **Scope**: A reference page in two halves. Part one is a method guide: what an agent evaluation measures (versus a model evaluation), the anatomy of one episode (task, trial, trajectory, outcome) with conceptual pseudocode, benchmark selection, grader types, the metrics table, cost and latency accounting, uncertainty, METR time horizons, a five-step design process, contamination versus grader gaming, and a reporting checklist. Part two is a 48-entry benchmark directory grouped into eight themes (text prediction, language understanding, knowledge/reasoning, assistant quality, interactive agents, harder/fresher test sets, agent capabilities in isolation, workplace workflows); each entry gives a representative task, scoring, what it measures, and source links. The note states its examples are invented illustrations, not released test items, and that the directory is a reference for task formats and scoring rules, not a ranking of increasingly intelligent systems.

### Evaluation dimensions

| Dimension | What it measures | Notes/caveats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Task success / resolution rate | Fraction of tasks or runs meeting the benchmark's completion checks | Report denominator and budget; checks may miss regressions |
| Outcome vs. procedural compliance | Final state versus required process (e.g. confirmation before a write) | A correct end state cannot prove consent came first; needs trajectory evidence |
| Cost per successful task | Total runtime cost incl. failed attempts divided by successful task trials | Undefined when no trial succeeds; do not divide by distinct task IDs solved at least once |
| Cost per attempted task | Total runtime cost divided by attempted trials | A low cost-per-success can hide poor task coverage; grading expense reported separately |
| Latency and parallelism | Wall-clock to a usable final result, and elapsed time vs. aggregate compute | Distinguish time-to-first-token; record timeouts and failures; parallel agents cut elapsed time while raising total cost |
| pass@k vs. pass^k | Probability at least one of k attempts succeeds vs. probability all k repeated trials succeed | Different events; pass@k needs a deployable selector, pass^k measures consistency |
| Tool-call correctness and efficiency | Function selection, argument grounding, call structure, turn count, milestones | A well-formed call can still be wrong; turn count is separate from task success (BFCL, ToolSandbox) |
| Memory recall and query latency | Answer correctness from retrieved evidence, and the cost of the memory system at that accuracy | Retrieving an answer-bearing session is intermediate, not correctness; V2 measures an accuracy-latency frontier |
| Safety under attack | Benign utility, utility under attack, targeted attack success rate | Refusing everything can cut attack success while making the agent useless |
| Coordination | Team task outcome plus a separate coordination score | Many messages do not imply useful division of work |
| Grader (judge) quality | Whether the automatic judge is right | Hand audit: the original judge was wrong in 96.4% of disagreements on Omni-MATH |
| Contamination and prompt sensitivity | Whether a task/solution was visible to training or the run, and how much of a score belongs to the harness | A date after a claimed cutoff reduces one risk only; MMLU-Pro cut prompt sensitivity from 4-5% to 2% across 24 prompt styles |
| Uncertainty | Variability across trials vs. task coverage | Use paired per-task differences; overlap of two score intervals is not a test |
| Calibration | Whether stated confidence tracks actual correctness | Accuracy and calibration are different measurements (HLE) |

- **Teaching points** (inference):
  - The note's central move is that a score without its protocol is not a measurement: benchmark version and split, permitted tools, budget, and grader must travel with every number.
  - The pass@k / pass^k contrast and the finite-sample estimators are the most teachable technical core: they show how to get consistency out of repeated trials without inventing tasks.
  - Cost and latency are treated as first-class outcome dimensions, and the paired A/B table is designed to stop the "one system dominates" reflex.
  - The benchmark directory is deliberately ordered by task format rather than difficulty, which supports the course's thesis that a suite choice is an evaluation-design decision.

## 2. Cost-effective agents
Source: `course/site/notes/cost-effective-agents.html` (mirror: `course/notes/notes_cost-effective-agents.md`)

- **Scope**: An organized design guide, not a paper list. It opens with the thesis that the relevant unit is the complete task (planning, tool use, retries, verification, human correction), then walks through measurement of the whole episode, tracing to find the expensive path, and then a series of technique sections, each with Mechanism, Best fit, and Measure. Every section closes with a "Sources" line of linked papers, and the page ends with a six-step protocol for running a fair cost experiment and a closing caveat that published savings are demonstrations under particular models, prices, workloads, and baselines. A companion "observed cost -> first lever to test" table and a "store -> cost avoided -> check before use" table give the page its decision-table character.

### Techniques

| Technique | What it optimizes | Mechanism | Cost/latency impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Measure the whole episode | Making cost attributable | Cost ledger over all model calls (main agent, planners, routers, compressors, workers, verifiers), priced uncached/cached input and output or reasoning tokens, plus tool fees, execution, memory storage and retrieval, and human review; self-hosted models use provisioned infrastructure cost | Adds bookkeeping; separates the deployed policy ledger from the offline ledger (teacher trajectories, fine-tuning, workflow search, evaluation, refresh) |
| Find the expensive path | Targeting effort | Per-step trace of model, role, input/output tokens, cache hits, tool calls, elapsed time, retries, outcome; group traces into successful, failed, timed-out | Diagnostic only; longest, most expensive, and typical traces can have different bottlenecks |
| Route model calls by expected value | Model price | Prompt-only router, cascade with verifier/confidence escalation, or budget-aware controller that sees trajectory state and remaining spend | Charge the router, failed cheap attempts, and recovery work; estimate incremental benefit, not "difficulty labels"; swapped-answer evaluation can misprice a trajectory |
| Small-model specialists | Cost of repeated narrow work | Train a smaller model on verified trajectories for a stable role (classify next tool, extract fields, summarize, plan a subgoal); small orchestrator calls strong models for exceptions | Setup costs: teacher calls, filtering, training, evaluation, hosting, refresh; break-even is lifetime per-call savings vs setup, and mistakes can shift into later steps |
| Budget reasoning, sampling, and search | Token and tool spend | Set deliberation/candidate/search expansion from task and evidence; stop sampling when candidates will not change the decision; give the agent a remaining budget for tokens and tools | A hard cap only ends work at the cap; more reasoning tokens can coexist with lower success; rerun on full tool-using trajectories before claiming savings |
| Remove model turns from routine control | Model calls per task | Put deterministic decisions in code; plans refer to future tool outputs so independent branches run together; code actions combine several tool operations; fixed pipelines for stable phases; smaller tool menus | Parallel work shortens the critical path only for independent branches and need not reduce total compute; blind batching can hide failures and force an expensive restart |
| Selective context (compression) | Repeated prompt tokens | Filter tool output to needed fields, retrieve instead of pasting, summarize finished branches, keep durable facts structured; preserve constraints, identifiers, unresolved errors, live state, original evidence | Peak active context vs cumulative billed input are different measures; discarded facts cost reacquisition calls; shortening a cached prefix can destroy a discount; the compressor's own calls belong in the ledger |
| Reuse facts, plans, executable skills | Repeated work | Choose the reusable object before the cache: fact, plan template, executable skill, or cached answer, each with preconditions and freshness checks | Memory writes, indexing, retrieval, consolidation, invalidation are real work; a long skill loaded every turn can cost more than on-demand retrieval; hit rate alone does not show correctness or payback |
| Pay only for useful collaboration | Multi-agent overhead | Reduce agents, messages, or edges; narrow what each role receives; cheaper model for routine roles; invoke a specialist only when its contribution is distinct | Fewer agents removes whole calls, sparse communication shortens prompts, role assignment changes per-call price; prune a role only after checking unique observation, independent check, or needed action |
| Amortize workflow and prompt optimization | Development cost | Search over prompts, demonstrations, tool interfaces, executable workflows on development tasks | Break-even is setup / net savings per deployed task; e.g. $2,000 setup saving $0.02 per task needs ~100,000 tasks; keep optimization-set and final-test results separate |
| Lower serving cost | Infrastructure cost | API: stable cached prefix, no timestamps or per-run state in it. Self-hosted: prefix and KV reuse, batching and scheduling, phase-aware serving, retention policy for multi-turn tool gaps | Quantization and speculative decoding improve throughput or latency but do not reduce logical tool calls; quantization can change decisions; a self-hosted speedup may not pass through to an API customer |
| Reduce waiting with a separate money budget | Latency | Run independent tools concurrently and schedule by dependency; speculate a likely next action and validate the draft before use | Shortens the critical path but can raise total work; read-only or idempotent work is easier to cancel; e.g. 20% faster at 30% more expensive has bought latency, not saved cost |
| Prevent expensive rework | Failure cost | Clear task specs and model-facing tool interfaces; targeted verifiers catch cheap-to-fix errors before more work is built on them | Verification pays when expected cost of caught errors exceeds the cost of the check; count human minutes, waiting, and review errors in the same ledger |

- **Teaching points** (inference):
  - The page is organized by "the cost it removes and the new costs it introduces", so every technique should be taught with its second-order cost, not only its saving.
  - The two decision tables (observed cost -> first lever; store -> cost avoided -> check before use) are the most slide-ready objects on the page and give students a lookup rather than a principle.

  - The note distinguishes five different measurements - token reduction, peak-context reduction, throughput, latency, dollars - and warns that published savings cannot be compared without naming which one.

## 3. Transformers and LLM foundations (reference note)
Source: `course/site/notes/transformers.html` (mirror: `course/notes/notes_transformers.md`)

- **Scope**: An optional reading guide assembled from the former October 19 LLM lecture, organized as ten groups of paper or model-report entries: Foundations, Tokenization, Architecture, Sparse and alternative architectures, The scaling-law argument, Data, Post-training, Reasoning, Evaluation, and Representative model reports. Each entry follows a fixed template (Background, Problem, Key idea, then Findings and/or Adoption where the source has them, then "Why it matters" for this course). The level is systems-oriented rather than mathematical: the stated reading rule is "read it for shapes, not for BLEU scores", and the recurring hinge is what one forward pass costs and what a serving stack must keep around. The note closes by saying the evaluation and model-report groups must be read together, and that the DeepSeek and Qwen3 reports sit with the mechanisms they introduced.

### Topic map

### Paper discussion format

- Two paper discussions: Oct 14 (agent safety and self-evolving harnesses) and Nov 16 (KV-cache optimization and model architectures). Paper presentations are optional and can earn up to four bonus percentage points each; students may volunteer individually or as a team; everyone reads for every discussion and comes with at least one thing you did not believe and one thing you would have done differently.
- Nine reading questions: what problem is being solved and who has it; what changed in hardware, workload, or models to make it worth solving now; the key insight in one sentence without jargon; what the design gives up; whether the baseline is fair and what baseline would have made the result look worse; what workload is evaluated and how representative; which numbers would change if model size, context length, or request rate doubled; what would break this in production; whether the result is still true on today's hardware or was a fact about a specific GPU generation.
- Meeting shape: 5 min instructor context, 20 min first presentation, 15 min open discussion, 20 min second presentation, 15 min open discussion; a three-paper meeting keeps the shape with shorter slots. The instructor supplies the opening context and leads when nobody volunteers.
- Presenter structure and evaluation lens: background and motivation ~5 min (the problem stated concretely with a number that shows it hurts, prior work and why it was insufficient, what changed to make this the right moment); key idea and design ~8 min (the idea in one sentence, the mechanism using the paper's own figures where good, and the one hard part where the idea meets reality); evaluation ~5 min (setup out loud - hardware, models, workloads - the two or three figures that matter, whether the baseline is the strongest alternative, what is not measured); discussion and takeaways ~2 min (what to keep, what has been superseded, two or three prepared open-ended questions). For AI systems papers specifically, check whether throughput gains cost tail latency, whether the request-length distribution is realistic, whether "GPU utilization" means occupancy or useful FLOPs, and whether the comparison holds accuracy fixed. Read Patterson's "How to Give a Bad Talk" (circa 1983, annotated by Mark D. Hill) and practice out loud against a clock.
- Presentation feedback: four criteria worth up to 1% each - content and understanding, slides and delivery, critical analysis, and discussion leadership; send slides to the instructor at least 24 hours before the session for feedback.
- Audience guidance and reading strategy: read the paper, not the abstract; arrive with one question written down; push on evaluation claims ("What was the baseline configured to do?"); connect papers to each other and to the lectures; disagree about ideas, not people; quality counts more than volume. Keshav's three passes: pass 1 (10-30 min) triage and mental map - title, abstract, introduction, section headings, every figure and caption, conclusion, references skim; pass 2 (1-2 hrs) understand and evaluate - architecture, assumptions about workload/hardware/model, experimental setup, and what the authors chose not to measure; pass 3 (deep dive) re-derive and stress-test - reconstruct the design first, sanity-check headline numbers against bandwidth/FLOPs/memory estimates, find the unstated assumption, ask what the paper would look like if the result were negative. Use pass 1 on everything, pass 2 on the papers assigned for discussion, pass 3 on the paper you are presenting and anything close to your own work.

- **Teaching points** (inference):
  - The discussion page teaches a repeatable critique procedure (three passes, nine questions, a fixed talk structure) that the course then reuses as the project and AI-stumper rubric.
  - The evaluation lens is written for exactly the systems claims students will make in Assignments 4 and 5 and the project, so it doubles as a grading rubric preview.
  - The three-pass strategy plus "reading fewer papers more carefully beats skimming many" frames reading as a budgeted skill, not a volume measure.
