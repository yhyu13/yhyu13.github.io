6. Essential mathematical ideas in the pictures

6.1 Fractions are quantities and relationships, not two whole numbers stacked together

The pictures deliberately use length, amount of water/material, and proportion. A fraction describes a quantity or a relationship between quantities; the numerator and denominator cannot be interpreted independently as ordinary whole-number magnitudes.

6.2 The whole and equal partitioning give a fraction meaning

Cutting land, boards, water, and supplies depends on two questions:

  1. What is the whole or reference unit?
  2. Are the parts equal in the relevant attribute—length, area, volume, quantity, or share?

Unequal pieces cannot automatically be named 1/4 merely because there are four pieces. Comparing fractions also requires attention to whether the reference wholes are the same.

6.3 Denominator and numerator play different roles

For a fixed whole:

  • the denominator says how many equal parts form the whole;
  • each equal part has unit fraction 1/b;
  • the numerator counts how many of those units are being considered.

For unit fractions with the same whole, a larger denominator means each piece is smaller. But “a larger denominator always means a larger fraction” is false, and denominator-only reasoning is not a valid general comparison strategy. 阿糊 embodies this whole-number misconception.

6.4 Fraction units support composition, decomposition, and equivalence

The 1/2 and 1/4 bridge boards make fractions physically composable and decomposable. Examples that the mechanic can represent include:

  • 1/2 = 2/4;
  • 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4;
  • a whole can be assembled in more than one way.

Equivalence is implicit in the pictures; it is not yet a fully specified instructional sequence.

6.5 Fractions are measures, and small differences matter

A board short by 1/8 makes the bridge fail. This treats a fraction as a measurable quantity, not only a shaded part of a picture. It also introduces precision: a representation and its physical result must agree.

6.6 Comparing fractions requires a common basis for comparison

The two-channel task contrasts 2/5 and 3/8:

2/5 = 16/40 = 0.400
3/8 = 15/40 = 0.375
Therefore 2/5 > 3/8, by 1/40 = 0.025.

The mathematical purpose is to challenge intuition based on only the numerator or denominator. Valid strategies include equivalent fractions, a common unit, visual models, or an appropriate benchmark—not a memorized denominator rule.

6.7 Fractions model equal sharing and division

Fair sharing of water and materials represents equal sharing. Depending on how the task is formalized, it can also support the relationship a ÷ b = a/b (b ≠ 0). Fairness is a mathematical constraint that can be tested, not merely a story theme.

6.8 Fractions can express ratio and proportion

The statement that length, amount, and proportion are all fractions, plus the orchard transfer task, points toward fractional rate/ratio reasoning: compare relative amounts rather than only absolute counts. The orchard task must define the relationship precisely—for example, harvested amount relative to total harvest—before it is mathematically evaluable.

6.9 Mathematics is modeled, tested, explained, and transferred

The core loop is mathematical modeling:

represent a situation with fractions → predict → act → observe a consequence → revise the model → explain why → use the idea in a new situation.

Teaching 阿糊 adds mathematical argumentation: the player must supply a reason that exposes why the misconception fails. Immediate transfer and delayed review are learning mechanisms, not additional fraction concepts.

7. What the pictures do not yet establish

The images do not fully specify:

  • a formal common-denominator algorithm;
  • fraction addition/subtraction beyond what the manipulatives imply;
  • fraction multiplication or division algorithms;
  • a number-line representation;
  • the exact definitions of whole, flow rate, field capacity, harvest proportion, and success criteria;
  • the grade placement of each task.

Therefore, the pictures should be read as a conceptual fraction-learning vertical slice, not a complete fractions curriculum.

8. Mathematical/product issues the derivative PRD must resolve

  1. Define the two-channel quantities. “Which field fills first” depends on field capacity, channel flow, and time, not on 2/5 and 3/8 in isolation. State exactly what each fraction means and ensure all other conditions are controlled.
  2. Make the 1/40 difference observable. A 2.5-percentage-point difference may be invisible in a short animation. Use a measurable cumulative scale, sufficient duration, equal field capacities, or a magnified visual readout without changing the underlying quantities.
  3. Define the orchard proportion. Specify numerator, denominator, reference whole, and whether this is fraction comparison or true ratio/proportion reasoning.
  4. Resolve the current scope conflict. The closest PRD excludes ratio/proportion from its G5 scope (lines 54 and 225). If the orchard task is genuine ratio/proportion work, the parent scope must be consciously revised rather than silently inherited.
  5. Do not swap the comparison task into the default G3 lesson without review. The current default trap is 1/8 versus 1/4 (§5.2 line 338); 2/5 versus 3/8 is a different comparison demand. Assign an appropriate band and representation.
  6. Separate physical truth from AI narration. The deterministic simulator should compute the result; an LLM must not decide whether a bridge holds or which field fills first.
  7. Keep AI roles bounded. The companion asks questions; 阿糊 follows a scripted misconception/evidence state machine; the coach uses minimal purposeful evidence; level templates and answer keys are prevalidated.
